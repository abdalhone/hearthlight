# Hearthlight — the lore of Halcyre

*The story bible for Hearthlight: the world, its magic and dungeons, its history, the village of Hearthvale and the eighteen people who live in these pages, Lyra's life, the stranger from the mountain, and the mysteries the game will pull on.*

A note on the shape of it: this is a world where people live close together because the open land is not safe. Every village is a lamp in a large dark room. Hearthlight is the story of one of those lamps, Hearthvale, of a woman named Lyra who was born there, and of a stranger the village carried down from a mountain.

### How to use this book

- **The author's canon comes first.** This is a world of magic and dungeons, with all kinds of magic. It is split into villages for safety, and Lyra lives in hers. She was born there to two old people. She is not the only one left: there are other people in the village, with lives of their own. The player came from a mountain: villagers found them there and took them in. Lyra lives for herself. She stops the player, runs or leaves only when that is better for her, or when staying is not worth it to her. Everything below is built to fit those facts.
- **This book replaces older text.** Where it disagrees with the built game, this book wins. Section 12 lists every line in the built game that it replaces and gives the new text for each.
- **Knowing a fact here does not mean a character knows it.** Every fact has an owner. Section 9 ends with a knowledge grid: for each secret, who knows it, who suspects it, who believes the false version, and what would make the holder tell. Section 7 says what Lyra knows of each person and which of their secrets stay hidden from her however well she knows them. A truth marked *hidden* belongs to the author and the game, and no character should say it until the game reveals it.
- **Each fact is told in one place.** The story of finding the stranger is told in Section 8, the Greywinter count in Section 9.6, and the Weeping Cut in Section 7. Other sections only point to them. Lines a character would say aloud are collected in Section 6 (*Says out loud*) and Section 11.
- Dates are given as "years ago", counted from the early autumn when the game begins.

## 1. The world

### Halcyre

The world is called **Halcyre**. The word is older than any kingdom. It comes from the speech of the Lanternwrights, the mage-builders of the deep past, and means something like *the lit hall*. They believed the world was one great hall and that their work was to keep its lamps burning. For a long time they did. Then the lamps went out one by one, and the hall became what it is now: a wide, beautiful, dangerous country where the light lives only in small, kept places.

The part of Halcyre where Hearthlight happens is the **Lowmarch**, the long middle country the King's Road crosses on its way from the sea to the mountains. To the south-west lies the coast, with the walled harbor town of Saltmere and, far out across the water, the Glasswater Isles. To the north rise the **Crownfells**, a wall of grey mountains. Their nearest and highest peak, the **Hollowcrown**, can be seen from Hearthvale's square on a clear morning, its broken summit white even in summer. Beyond the Crownfells lies the **Pale Reach**, a land of snow and aurora that most maps do not bother to draw. Between the coast and the mountains are valleys, forests, moors, marshes and rivers, and scattered through them like coals in a dark grate are the villages.

### Why the world is separated into villages

Because the magic in Halcyre is loose.

Long ago the Lanternwrights held the world's magic in a single great working they called **the Song**, a ward over everything that kept wild power as still as water in a cistern. When the Song broke (the people of the Lowmarch call that time **the Unlighting**), the magic did not vanish. It spilled. Now it moves over the open land the way weather does, and it is called simply **the Drift**.

The Drift is not evil. It is not anything. It is power with no purpose moving over the land, and it changes what it touches. Villagers talk about it the way sailors talk about the sea, with a word for each of its moods:

- **Glimmer** is the mildest drift-weather: a sparkling haze at dusk that makes lamps burn blue and dogs howl. It does little harm, and children love it.
- **The grey** is a fog that makes things forget themselves. Fences forget to stand. People forget errands and names, and if it lasts long enough, they forget why they ever kept their hearths lit. A long grey is how villages die.
- **Hollow wind** is a dry, cold wind that steals warmth. It puts out candles and chills the blood. It leaves the strong shivering and the weak dead in their beds.
- **Brightfall** is the strangest: light that falls like snow on still winter nights. Where it settles, odd things grow. Flowers bloom in frost. Beasts give birth out of season. Sometimes people do too.
- **The turning** is what villagers call the Drift's effect on animals. Turned wolves grow bold and hunt in bigger packs. Turned boars grow vicious. Still water that sits too long in drift-weather thickens, stirs and crawls away as a **slime**.

Then there are the **dungeons** (Section 3): places underground where the old magic pooled. They wake and sleep on their own slow tides and send out things that should not walk in daylight.

No one can live in the Drift for long. So people live inside **wards**.

### How villages protect themselves

A ward is a boundary the Drift cannot easily cross. Inside it, weather is just weather, wolves are just wolves, and water stays water. Every village in the Lowmarch exists because it has one, and most are built the same way, from pieces of the old world and the habits of the new:

- **The wardstone.** At the heart of every ward is a stone the Lanternwrights shaped and left behind, still humming faintly with a fragment of the old Song. Some are standing stones and some are lintels. Hearthvale's lies at the bottom of the well in the town square and is called the **Wellstone**. Nobody alive can make a wardstone, so every village that has one was built where one was found.
- **Kept flame.** On its own a wardstone is cold and weak. It is fed by fire that is kept with intent, never allowed to go out, and offered to it. Every household keeps one **family flame** on the altar of the village chapel. Together these flames are called **the Hearthlight**, and they hold the ward up. A flame covers its whole household: everyone who lives under that family's roof and name, however many. But a stone can bear only so many flames before it cracks, and that number is the most important secret a village keeps.
- **Letting people in.** That makes letting people in a question of arithmetic. A household that comes in from outside can be given its own place on the stone, by setting its road-candle on the altar as a new flame, but only if the stone has a place to spare. Otherwise a village household can take the newcomers *under* its own flame as kin. Then the stone carries nothing new, but the household carries them instead: roof, bread, and the law's view that their deeds are now the household's deeds. The first way costs the stone. The second costs the neighbors. Every hard night in the Lowmarch's history has been an argument between the two.
- **The keepers.** A **wardwright** is a mage who binds herself to a stone, tends it, mends it, and feels it in her own body. A **chapel keeper** tends the family flames and the rites. A **lamplighter** walks the lamps at dusk, because a ward is weakest where it is dark. A **wardrunner** goes outside to check the edges, read the weather and bring warning.
- **Customs.** Ward-chalk goes on every threshold, renewed at the new moon. Cold iron nails go over doors. A bell's sound marks how far the ward reaches: in Hearthvale, the ward runs about as far as the chapel bell can be heard on a still day, which villagers call **the bell-reach**. The thin outer band where the ward fades is **the hem**. Nobody leaves the ward alone. Nobody lets a hearth go out. Anyone let in from outside spends their first night under the chapel roof before they sleep anywhere else. And nobody spills blood inside a ward if they can help it, because violence inside the bell-reach thins it, and everyone knows that.

### Travel and roads

Most people in the Lowmarch never go farther than the hem of their own ward. Those who do travel carry a **road-candle**: a flame from their home hearth in a shuttered lantern, which keeps a small circle of ward around them as long as it burns. They travel in groups, by day, in the **still weeks** when the weather-wise say the Drift is quiet, and they never camp where they cannot see a waystone.

The **King's Road** runs from Saltmere on the coast to the foot of the Crownfells. A king laid it four hundred years ago, dreaming of joining the villages into one country again. Along its length he raised or restored **waystones**: tall pillars with a lamp niche at the top. A waystone with a lit lamp throws a small ward across the road, enough for a caravan to shelter under for a night. Perhaps one in three still works, and two more went dark this summer. The rest are dead stone, and the stretches between live ones are where carts disappear.

Near Hearthvale, the road comes in from the west past the marsh of the Sunken Ruins and the fields of Reed Farm. It runs along the village's southern edge and crosses the stream at the old bridge (the stream is the river Bright, just out of Mirror Lake). Past the bridge it splits. The **Bright Road** follows the river south-east to Brightwater. The King's Road bends north around the far shore of Mirror Lake to the **Cinderwick fork**, where the oil track turns east into the hills toward Cinderwick. North of the fork, carts stopped going years ago. The road thins into a track that climbs for three days to Owlsend under the mountains, and after that into a rumor. The bandits camp in the hills north-east of the lake, above the fork, where they can watch both roads.

Merchants like Garrick Hale, wardrunners like Hess Morrow, pilgrims, couriers and the desperate are the only people who travel regularly. A letter takes weeks. News comes with the carts and is old by the time it is told.

### Other villages

About thirty villages in the Lowmarch still keep a ward. They sit a few days apart, and nobody knows how many dead ones lie between them. Hearthvale deals with five of them, and the rest are just names. People speak of other places the way you speak of distant cousins: with affection, suspicion and a fund of half-true stories.

- **Brightwater** is a river town three days south-east by the Bright Road, or two days down the river by the ferry that used to leave from Mirror Lake. It is big enough to have two chapels and a market, and its ward is strong enough to have walls inside it. Tobias's wife June is there "visiting her sister". People who go to Brightwater for a season have a way of staying.
- **Saltmere** is the harbor town on the coast, twelve days south-west, the nearest thing the Lowmarch has to a city. It has a guild of delvers, a mint that once struck coins for kings, a library, and a sea-wall carved with the names of drowned sailors. Garrick's mother and brother live there. So does Lyra's oldest friend, Bryn Holloway, who is a scribe there now.
- **Cinderwick** is a mining village in the eastern hills, at the end of the oil track. Its wardstone sits in a coal seam that has been burning, slowly and on purpose, for two hundred years. Cinderwick folk are black-handed, proud and blunt, and they make the best lamp-oil in the Lowmarch. Every street lamp in Hearthvale burns it.
- **Owlsend** is the last village before the mountains: small, stubborn and poor, three days up the north track. On clear nights its lamps could be seen from the shoulder of the Hollowcrown. Hess Morrow says that at midsummer they could not (Section 9.7).
- **Brindlecombe** is a dead village a day and a half west, its houses still standing with their doors open. Its ward failed nineteen years ago in a winter-long grey that the Lowmarch remembers as **the Greywinter**. What happened at Hearthvale's ward line that winter is something the older villagers do not talk about (Section 9.6).
- **The Glasswater Isles** lie far to the south-west across the sea. The sand there is like crushed glass, and the lagoon glows on summer nights. No one in Hearthvale has been there except Maren Ashgrove, who was born there.

## 2. Magic

Halcyre has every kind of magic, because magic is everywhere: loose in the Drift, pooled in the dungeons, humming in the old stones, sleeping in certain plants and certain people. What differs is how people reach it, what it asks of them, and what their neighbors think of them for using it. Villagers have no single word for magic. They have a word for each kind, and opinions about all of them.

### Hearthcraft — the magic of kept flame and boundaries

**How it works.** Hearthcraft is the craft of wards: of saying *here, and not past here*. It works through fire kept on purpose, lines drawn on purpose, and stones that remember the Song. A household chalk-mark over a door is hearthcraft. So is the chapel's ring of family flames, and so is a wardwright's binding to the Wellstone.

**Cost and risk.** Hearthcraft binds the one who uses it to a place. A family that lets its chapel flame go out feels it: the house goes cold in a way no fire fixes until the flame is relit. A wardwright pays the most. Once she is bound to a stone, she feels every crack in the ward as an ache in her own bones, and she cannot go more than a day's walk from the stone without falling sick. In return she gives the stone her own steadiness, and a married stone bears four or five flames more than it would alone. It is a marriage, and villagers call it one: *marrying the stone*. The old books add something few people have read: a stone married unwillingly holds badly.

**Who and how common.** Everyone does a little of it. A real wardwright is rare; perhaps one village in four has one. Hearthvale's was Maren Ashgrove. Since she vanished, Sister Aveline has fed the stone with the chapel rites, but Aveline is not a mage, and the ward is thinning (Section 9.4).

**How villagers feel.** Hearthcraft is the only magic everyone trusts. It is also the one they most expect someone else to carry for them.

### Lumencraft — the Lamp-art

**How it works.** Lumencraft is the art of shaping light into force. A lumencrafter gathers the warmth in her own body, kindles it into light, and gives the light a shape: a bolt, a shield, a lamp that hangs in the air, a ring that holds. It is the most mage-like of the magics, the one in the stories, and it has to be studied. It takes years of teaching, star charts, breath-work and a great deal of failing.

**Cost and risk.** The light is made from the caster's own warmth. Every spell takes a little heat from her body. A long fight leaves a lumencrafter blue-lipped and shaking, a state called **lamp-chill**. If she pushes past it she **gutters**: she faints, cold as stone, and if no one warms her she may not wake. That is why lumencrafters love fires, wool, strong tea and moonpetals. It is also why the good ones carry a **heartglass**: a glow crystal set into a staff, which stores warmth the way a bank stores coin, so she can spend from it instead of from her own blood.

**Who and how common.** Rare. A village is lucky to see one in a generation. The towns have a few and the Saltmere delvers' guild has more. Most came to it the way Lyra did: the light woke in them by itself, and someone who knew the Art happened to be close enough to teach them before they hurt themselves.

**How villagers feel.** Admiration with a little fear in it. A lumencrafter can light a lamp on a hollow-wind night, mend a wound, and burn a wolf out of the dark. If she is angry, she can do the same to a person.

**Where Lyra's magic sits.** Lyra is a lumencrafter, trained by Maren from the age of eleven. She has three workings:

- **Sparks** (the game's arcane bolts) are the simplest lumencraft there is: light thrown hard enough to strike. Lyra's burn violet, which is unusual; Maren's were white. Each one costs a breath of warmth.
- **Lending warmth** (her healing spell) is the hardest. She pours warmth and light into someone else's body to speed its own mending. It costs her more than anything else she does, because she is giving away exactly what she is made of. Maren told her: *never lend more than you can live without.* Lyra learned that harder and earlier than Maren wanted (Section 7, the Weeping Cut).
- **The Stillring** (Binding Light) is Maren's own invention, and it is what makes her school unusual. It is lumencraft shaped by hearthcraft: a ward drawn around one body instead of one village. A ring of violet light closes around someone's feet, and for a few heartbeats the world tells them *not past here*. It does not hurt and it does not last. Maren called it *the polite way to say stop*, and Lyra uses it for exactly that: to make someone stop, so that she can choose what happens next.

Her staff is ash wood shod in iron. It was re-shod three years ago after the old ferrule split on the lake ice, and the new ferrule was Tilly Ashby's first paid work. It is tipped with a heartglass that Maren gave her on her sixteenth birthday; Maren had taken it from the upper halls of the Sunken Ruins, with the Moot's leave, in her first year in Hearthvale. When the staff glows at night, that is the crystal giving back warmth.

### Greenwit — hedge-magic and the quickened herb

**How it works.** Some plants hold magic. Moonpetals open only at night and drink moonlight. Silverleaf knits flesh. Blue ruin-lichen glows when you breathe on it. Lindenflower honey keeps for a hundred years. Greenwit is the craft of knowing these plants, gathering them at the right hour, and brewing them so their magic wakes in a body. A greenwitch does not cast; she prepares. Mira Fennick would never call herself a mage. She is the best greenwitch in the valley.

**Cost and risk.** The risks are in the gathering and in mistakes. Moonpetals grow along the edge of Whisperwood, from inside the ward out into the hem, and the biggest blooms grow farthest out, where wolves walk at night. Drift-touched herbs look the same as clean ones and can sour a potion into poison. The worst risk is that a greenwitch who gathers in drift-weather brings a little of the weather home. Herbs cut in glimmer make a house's lamps burn blue for a week. Herbs cut in the grey make the brewer forget. Rosalind Fennick gathered silverleaf all through the Greywinter because people needed it. She spent her last years forgetting small things, then names, then Mira's. Mira does not gather in drift-weather, for anyone.

**Who and how common.** Every village has someone who does it, and every grandmother knows a little.

**How villagers feel.** Gratitude, and a faint condescension they would deny. Greenwit is "just herbs" until you need it.

Moonpetals warm cold hands, ease lamp-chill, and soothe a guttered mage back to waking. Maren bought Mira's strongest moonpetal salve before she went into the ruins, "for cold hands in deep places". Elda puts crushed moonpetal in her honey cakes, which is why Lyra, who spends warmth for a living, cannot get enough of them, though she has not yet worked out why.

### Forgesong — iron that remembers

**How it works.** Some smiths, mostly without meaning to, put something into iron as they work it: a ring, a note, a stubbornness. A forgesung blade bites slimes and turned beasts as if they were cloth. A forgesung nail over a door holds a ward-chalk line through a hollow wind. The old smiths say you do it by listening to the iron as it cools and refusing to strike until it is ready. That is exactly what Bram Ironhand does, and he would be offended to hear it called magic.

**Cost and risk.** The iron keeps what the smith felt while it was sung. A blade forged in grief is heavy in anyone's hand. One forged in anger turns in the grip. The old smiths also say forgesingers go quiet, because the iron takes the words, and Bram, who cannot say what he feels, writes it at night instead. He has not forged a blade he was proud of since Hanna died. The stakes that seal the Weeping Cut were forged in a fury, and that is why they hold.

**Who and how common.** A handful of families pass it down like a cough. The Ironhands are one of them.

**How villagers feel.** They don't think about it. They just know Bram's blades are better.

The stone of the Sunken Ruins rings like an anvil when struck, and Bram is right to think that means something. The Lanternwrights forged as well as sang.

### The Old Song — Lanternwright songcraft

**How it works.** No one knows anymore. The Lanternwrights sang their workings into stone, and the stones still hum: wardstones, waystones, and the great stone rings called **waygates** that once crossed the world in a single step. Their script survives carved on ruins, and a few scholars, Maren among them, can read a little of it. A few fragments of the Song can still be hummed or chanted, and they still do something.

**Cost and risk.** Unknown, which is the risk. Maren believed the Song does not cost the singer warmth, as lumencraft does, but *place*: whoever sings deep enough becomes part of what is sung.

**Who and how common.** Almost no one. Maren. Perhaps now the stranger from the mountain (Section 8), though nobody knows that yet.

**How villagers feel.** Uneasy reverence. The Song made the world safe once, and then it failed.

### Driftcraft — the brightborn

**How it works.** It is not a craft. Some people are touched by the Drift: born under brightfall, or caught in glimmer as children. They come away with something: a knack for finding water, eyes that see in the dark, hair the color of a night sky, or magic that wakes too strong and too early. They are called **brightborn** or, less kindly, **drift-touched**.

**Cost and risk.** No one knows what the Drift takes with one hand while it gives with the other. Brightborn gifts are wild and hard to steer. Some brightborn burn out young. Some are simply a little odd all their lives.

**Who and how common.** Uncommon, and mostly hidden, because of how villagers feel about them.

**How villagers feel.** Suspicion. The old women make warding signs. In bad years, villages have put brightborn children outside the ward. Hearthvale has not done that in living memory, and Petra Ashby would like to see them try.

For twenty-four years Hearthvale has whispered that Lyra Ambrel is brightborn. She was born half a year after the Brightfall winter, to a mother of fifty-two, and her hair grew in blue. Lyra knows people whisper it and has decided not to care. Whether it is true is one of the mysteries in Section 9.

### Gravelight — the dungeons' own magic

**How it works.** Deep in waking dungeons, the pooled magic grows something like hunger, and it wears whatever it finds. It wears the bones of the dead and walks them as **skeletons**. It wears darkness and flies it as **bats**. Its light is cold and blue, and it is called **gravelight**. A few people have tried to learn to use it. The stories about them all end badly.

**Cost and risk.** Everything. Gravelight is not given. It is borrowed from something that wants it back.

**How villagers feel.** Horror. It is the one magic every Moot in the Lowmarch forbids.

Hearthvale has two faces of it close by: the thing with too many bones that came up out of the Weeping Cut five years ago, and the blue light that has moved in the Sunken Ruins since midsummer. It also has one man tempted by it, though nobody in the village knows that yet (Section 9.3).

### Small charms — everybody's magic

Then there is the magic everyone does without thinking of it as magic: knots tied in a sweetheart's hair-ribbon, salt across a sill, a bell on a goat, humming to the fish (Corran Dell swears by it), a spoon of honey left out on Longwatch night, a word said over bread, a ribbon tied to the heart oak in Whisperwood. Most of it does nothing at all, and some of it does a little. All of it makes people braver in the dark, which in Halcyre is half of what any magic is for.

## 3. Dungeons

### What they are

When the Song broke, the world's magic spilled, and like spilled water it ran downhill. Most of it became the Drift and moves over the land. But some ran down into the low places: caves, mine-workings, cellars, the vaults of drowned temples and, above all, the buried works of the Lanternwrights, which were made to hold magic and still do. There it pooled, and the pools became **dungeons**: places where magic gathers so thickly that it starts to behave like something alive.

The Lowmarch has dozens. Most are small and asleep. A few are large and famous: the **Deepvein** under the Cinderwick hills, whose crystal-seams the Border Wars were fought over, and the **Drowned Choir**, which the Saltmere delvers work at low tide. Hearthvale has two near it: the **Sunken Ruins**, and a small one called the **Weeping Cut** under Stonecut Quarry. The Moot sealed the Cut with Bram's iron stakes five years ago (Section 7 tells why).

### How they wake and sleep

Dungeons breathe, very slowly. For years, sometimes for generations, a dungeon sleeps. Its halls are cold, its stone is quiet, and the brave and the greedy can walk in and carry out crystals and old coins. Then something wakes it: strong drift-weather, a disturbance at its heart, or someone opening a door that was shut on purpose. Waking takes weeks, and it has signs: lights moving inside at night, stone that hums when you stand still, slimes in nearby pools, animals that will not graze nearby. A waking dungeon pushes its creatures out into the world. A fully woken one is a place no sensible person enters.

### What lives in them

Dungeons do not breed creatures. They make them from whatever they have.

- **Slimes** are water that sat too long in magic. They gather in dungeon pools, in quarry rain-holes, along lake shores where the ward is thin, and in meadow puddles after drift-weather. They are slow, stupid and acidic, and their gel makes a fine burn salve.
- **Bats** are the dungeon's darkness given wings. They pour out of waking dungeons at night.
- **Skeletons** are the dead, worn like clothes. A dungeon's hunger finds bones (of pilgrims, delvers, the old keepers) and walks them. They are not the people they were and they remember nothing. They guard, they walk the halls at night, and their joints rattle against the stone.
- **Glow crystals** are not creatures but the dungeon's own light, cooled and set hard. They grow where magic pools longest, and they are the most valuable thing a dungeon gives: a heartglass for a mage's staff, a store of warmth for a ward in a hard winter, a treasure for a smith. Bram pays better for crystals than for ore, and he is right to.

### What delving means

To **delve** is to go into a dungeon on purpose and come out with something. In the towns it is a trade with a guild, rules and a widows' fund. In the villages it is forbidden without the Moot's leave, because a delver who wakes a dungeon wakes it for everyone. Delvers go in pairs or more, with road-candles, rope, a mage if they can afford one, and an agreement about when to turn back. The old delvers' rule is: *the deep pays the one who leaves first.*

### The Sunken Ruins

To the village, the Sunken Ruins are an old walled temple half swallowed by the marsh south-west of Hearthvale: fallen pillars, blue lichen, and stone that rings when you strike it. Sister Aveline says it was "a temple to a fire no one remembers", sealed by its own people. As far as that goes, she is right.

**The wall and the gate.** The ruins' wall is twice a man's height and sheer, with the marsh at its foot. It had one low stretch on the marsh side. That is where Maren went over at midsummer, and that same night it slumped into the marsh and became a sinkhole of black water. The only other way in is the iron gate on the road side, with the old ruins-keeper's lodge beside it. A hundred and ten years ago three pilgrims went below the shrine in the upper halls and never came back, and the Moot locked the gate at the chapel's asking. The weathered sign still says *sealed by order of the chapel*. The key hung in the empty lodge until this spring, when the bandits took it. Lyra has climbed the high wall once, alone, on a rope, and knows better than anyone why the gate matters: a gate is a way out you can run through, and a rope is not (Section 7).

**What they truly are.** The ruins were a **verse-house** of the Lanternwrights, one of the places where a part of the Song was kept burning. Its "forgotten fire" was the **Undying Lamp**, a flame sung into stone. Below it, at the bottom of the temple's stair, is a **waygate**: a ring of stone that is not dead. When the Song broke, the verse-house sank into the marsh. Its keepers did not flee: they sealed the temple from the inside and went down. What became of them is the first hidden truth in Section 9.

For centuries the ruins slept. Pilgrims left lamp-pennies at the shrine in the upper halls until the gate was locked. The ruins began to wake at midsummer, on the night Maren Ashgrove went in. Blue gravelight moves in them after dark, skeletons walk the halls, bats pour out at dusk, and the stone hums low and slow, like something breathing. In the deepest room, at the foot of the stair, Maren left her journal.

### The Weeping Cut

The Cut is a small, old working under the north face of Stonecut Quarry. Quarrymen broke into it two hundred years ago, heard it dripping, and called it the Weeping Cut. It slept for most of those two hundred years. Five years ago three young people went in without leave and woke it (Section 7). The Moot sealed it with iron stakes Bram forged, and the stakes hold the door. They do not hold the water that seeps out under it, which is the hidden reason the quarry pools have slimes in them (Section 9.4).

## 4. History

Hearthvale counts years from its own founding, and the rest of the Lowmarch counts them however it likes. The table below is the history people know. Where the truth is different, the entry points to Section 9.

| When | What happened |
|---|---|
| **The Age of Lanterns** (beyond counting) | The Lanternwrights sing the Song and hold the world's magic still. They raise wardstones, waystones and waygates, build verse-houses where parts of the Song are kept burning, and call the world Halcyre, the lit hall. Old stories say the Song had singers who gave their lives to it, and that the Lanternwrights honored those singers above kings. |
| **The Unlighting** (about a thousand years ago) | The Song breaks. The Lowmarch says nobody knows why (Section 9.8). Magic spills over the land as the Drift and runs down into the dungeons. The verse-houses sink, and the Sunken Ruins go down into the marsh with their keepers sealed inside. The Lanternwrights vanish from the world. |
| **The Hearth Years** (the centuries after) | Survivors gather around fires near the old stones and learn, by trial and grief, that kept flame wakes a wardstone. The first villages are built, and the Hearth faith begins: *a candle does not ask the dark for permission.* |
| **The Crowned Road** (about 400 years ago) | King Aldwin the Lampbearer, of a line out of Saltmere, lays the King's Road from the coast to the Crownfells and relights its waystones. For a hundred and fifty years the Lowmarch is something like a kingdom, divided into three counties whose names Saltmere people still use. |
| **Hearthvale founded** (312 years ago) | A caravan caught in a hollow wind shelters in the lee of a hill beside the road. ## 5. Hearthvale

### Its founding and its ward

Hearthvale grew up around a kettle-fire that would not go out, a well with a wardstone at the bottom, and a chandler who kept the dark back with candles. It has kept all three at its heart for three hundred and twelve years. The Ember Kettle's hearth is Gerda Mead's fire, never once allowed to die, and on Kindling Day every family in the valley relights its hearth from it. The well in the town square still gives sweet water, with a taste of iron and moss. On very still nights, if you lean over it, you can hear the Wellstone hum. The Chapel of the Hearth stands on the north side of the square, a stone's throw from the well, and the family flames burn on its altar. Every dusk an Ambrel lights the lamps around the square and along the lanes, as one has since Ansel Ambrel's day.

The ward's bell-reach covers the village, Reed Farm, the near edge of Whisperwood, the western shore of Mirror Lake and most of South Meadow. Stonecut Quarry sits in the hem, and the village blames the thin hem for the slimes in its rain-pools. The Bandit Camp, the deep forest, the far side of the lake and the Sunken Ruins are all outside the ward.

Thirty-eight family flames burn on the altar, and anyone can count them. The Wellstone can bear forty-one on its own. While Maren was married to it, it bore forty-five. Only the Reeve, the chapel keeper, the lamplighter and the wardwright know the number forty-one. So Hearthvale has three places to spare, and with Maren gone, only three people in the village know it.

### Its people

About a hundred and fifty people live in Hearthvale, in thirty-seven households plus the Wayhouse: thirty-eight flames in all. Eighteen of them speak in these pages, and those are the eighteen the game shows. The rest are background: neighbors to nod to, gossip about, and buy barrels from.

### Customs and festivals

- **Dusk bell and lamplighting.** At sunset the chapel bell rings once and the lamplighter begins his round. Children who are out when the bell rings run home. It is the oldest rule in the village.
- **Threshold night.** Anyone the village lets in from outside spends their first night under the chapel roof, beside the family flames, before they sleep anywhere else inside the ward. The stranger's threshold night lasted the whole of the long sleep.
- **Hearthwatch.** Someone brought in from outside is not left alone under a roof for their first season. A villager takes the hearthwatch: they hold the house key, look in every day, and sleep under the same roof when they judge it right. The Moot pays the watcher a stipend. The watcher can give it up at any time by handing the key back to the Reeve, and the captain of the watch then keeps the watch from the guardhouse until someone else takes it. Nobody is ever obliged to stay.
- **Joining flames.** Two households marry by joining flames. Each brings its candle to the altar, a new candle is lit from both, and the two old ones are allowed to go out together. It is the oldest kind of wedding in the valley, and it frees a place on the stone. Petra is taking bets on whether Hess and Sabeth will join flames at this year's Sheaf Moot.
- **Kindling Day** (first thaw). Every hearth in the valley is put out at dawn and relit from the Ember Kettle's fire, and the whole village carries coals home in clay pots. Children may carry the Kettle's coal for the first time at seven.
- **Moonpetal Night** (late summer). The village children go with the watch to the Moonpetal Glade, out in the hem of Whisperwood, and gather moonpetals by their glow, and the best bloom goes on the altar. Pip has been talking about next year's since this year's.
- **The Sheaf Moot** (harvest, ten days off). The last sheaf from Reed Farm is burned on the well-cap as an offering to the Wellstone. The Hearthmoot meets in the Ember Kettle to settle the year's business, and there is a dance in the square, a hammer-throw, and a ribbon for the biggest turnip. Mira hides from the dance. This year the Moot has two hard questions to decide: what to do about the bandits, and whether to ask Lyra Ambrel, formally and in front of everyone, to marry the stone.
- **Longwatch** (midwinter). The longest night. No one sleeps. The whole village keeps the chapel flames together, tells stories and sings the old songs, and leaves a spoon of honey on the doorstep for anyone still on the road. Just before dawn, the lamplighter's star, which villagers call **Lyra**, stands over the Hollowcrown.

### Its economy

Hearthvale feeds itself and sells a little. Reed Farm grows turnips and wheat. Stonecut Quarry gives building stone, and iron ore for the Ironhand forge. Corran Dell fishes Mirror Lake. Wenna Ambrel's hives along the edge of Whisperwood give lindenflower honey and wax, and Hobb turns the wax into the chapel's candles. Mira Fennick brews potions and salves. Sabeth Coll dyes and sews. Hess Morrow brings in pelts and venison. The Holloways make barrels, and Nan Pike weaves. Elda Thornbury feeds everyone, and the Ember Kettle is where coin changes hands.

Garrick Hale's cart is the village's only regular road to the outside. Once a month he goes down the Bright Road to Brightwater, once before winter he goes up to the fork and east along the oil track to Cinderwick, and twice a year he goes all the way to Saltmere. He takes out honey, wax, blades, potions, cloth and pelts. He brings back salt, needles, dyes, news, lies, and lamp-oil. Every street lamp in Hearthvale burns Cinderwick oil, while the family flames burn Hobb's beeswax. Since the bandits began tolling the fork, oil costs half again what it did, a cart of the chapel's oil crates never arrived, and Hobb is trimming the wicks short. Coin is copper and silver, and some of it is very old.

### Its rules

Hearthvale is governed by the **Hearthmoot**: the head of every household that keeps a flame in the chapel, meeting in the Ember Kettle's common room when there is business. It elects a **Reeve** to keep its ledgers and speak for it. The Reeve is Alder Ashby. Captain Rowan Vale keeps the watch and the peace. The laws are few and old:

1. Keep your flame. A household whose chapel flame goes out through neglect loses its voice in the Moot for a year.
2. No one goes outside the ward alone, and no one goes past the wall of the Sunken Ruins at all.
3. No blood inside the bell-reach. Striking a neighbor is the worst common crime in the village, because it thins the ward for everyone. Knocking someone senseless is worse. The punishment is the Moot's to set, and more than once it has been *outside*.
4. No delving without the Moot's leave.
5. Strangers keep threshold night and stay under hearthwatch for a season.
6. What the Moot knows of the ward's numbers is not repeated outside the Moot.

Everything else the Moot does is everybody's business, and everybody makes it so. The question of asking Lyra to marry the stone has been chewed over at every hearth in the valley.

This summer Lyra climbed the ruins wall alone and broke the second law twice over. Rowan put it before the Moot himself. The Moot fined her ten silver, which she paid the same day, and fined Hess one silver for lending her the rope. Lyra told the Moot she would do it again if she had to, and that next time she would tell the captain first. Rowan has not forgotten either half of that.

### Its worries right now

The bandits are the worry people say aloud. They have taken Tobias's sheep, a cart of Bram's blades, the chapel's oil and two lives, and they hold the key to the ruins gate. The ruins are the worry people say quietly: the lights, the rattling, the bats, and Maren gone into them. The ward is the worry almost no one says at all. Without a wardwright it is thinning, the slimes are in the quarry, the wolves are bolder every night, and winter is coming. Then there is the stranger, who sleeps in the Wayhouse and has no past; the question the Moot means to put to Lyra; June Reed's long visit; the oil; and Hess's report that Owlsend's lamps have gone dark. And the harvest must come in, whatever else happens.

### What a day sounds like

Before dawn: Rowan's boots on the bounds path, and the creak of the guardhouse door. Then Duchess, Tobias's brown hen, who believes the sun waits for her. The chapel bell at sunrise, once. Bram's anvil starting up, steady enough to set the hours by, and Tilly's lighter hammer answering it, a little off the beat. Elda's kettle screaming in the Kettle's kitchen, and Elda's voice over it. Bees along the edge of the wood. Pip shouting for Biscuit. Corran's tuneless humming drifting up from the dock. Garrick calling his wares to nobody in particular, and Elda calling the price of her bread one copper lower. In mid-afternoon the Reed wheat hisses in the wind, and Tobias apologizes to it. At sunset comes the dusk bell, the scrape of Hobb Ambrel's ladder against the first lamp-post, and doors closing all along the lane. Then the Kettle's common room: Hobb and Corran arguing over a game of stones, someone singing badly, Elda laughing. Late at night, a wooden flute plays somewhere near the guardhouse, and most people blame Wys. Last of all comes the Wellstone humming at the bottom of the well, which you only hear when everything else is quiet. Lyra can hear it from anywhere on the square.

## 6. The people

Eighteen people live in these pages: the eight villagers the game already knows, deepened, and ten more. They talk to each other when no one is listening, and this section is what they talk about: who they love, who they owe, what they are hiding, what they say out loud, and what they make of Lyra and of the stranger from the mountain. Ages are as of this autumn. Each profile has the same parts:

- **Home and days**: where the game can find them.
- **Says out loud**: their open opinions on the two Sheaf Moot questions (the bandits, and the stone), their pet topics, a running gag, a petty grievance, and what they are looking forward to this week. This is the material for everyday talk.
- **Greeting**: how they say hello, and how they answer "oh hello, how are you?"
- **Secret**: what they do *not* say. Section 9's knowledge grid says who else knows it and what would make them tell.

### Hobb Ambrel — chandler and lamplighter, 79
*Look:* tall and stooped like a lamp-post in a wind, white tufts of hair, beeswax on his cuffs, spectacles pushed up on his head where he cannot find them, and the long brass-hooked lamplighter's pole always in hand.

**Who he is.** In forty-one years Hobb has never once been late lighting a lamp, and he is late for everything else. He is gentle, slow and dry, and deaf in one ear when it suits him. He did his duty every evening of his life, and very late in it he has begun to wonder what else he might have done.

**His story.** He was born an Ambrel, which means born to the ladder. At twenty-three he broke with family custom and walked the King's Road for fifteen years as a road-warden, carrying road-candles for caravans, first beside Abel Ironhand, Bram's father, and then alone. He came home at thirty-eight when his father died, took up the ladder, and married Wenna, who had waited for him. They lost three babies, Ansel, Bee and little Jem, and none of them saw a second winter. Then, when he was fifty-five, came Lyra. In the Greywinter the Moot asked him to count, and fourteen children were let in "on the lamplighter's count" (Section 9.6). He took the eldest of them, Sabeth, under his own flame.

**Home and days.** The chandlery on Lamplight Lane. Mornings at the dipping-vat with Wenna's wax. Afternoons in his chair, "resting his eyes". The lamp-round at dusk. Evenings at the Kettle, playing stones with Corran.

**Wound.** The Greywinter. For nineteen years he has believed the grey made him miscount.
**Want.** To see Lyra live by her own light. To see the sea once before he dies.
**Secret.** He remembers, faintly, a bigger number than fourteen, and he cannot trust the memory. He has never told Wenna that, or that he recounts the chapel's flames every night from the top of his ladder. Also, his eyes are failing at dusk. Lyra has been lighting the far lamps on Lamplight Lane before he gets to them, and he pretends not to notice.

**Says out loud.** *Bandits:* "Hungry men are still men. Somebody ought to ask what they want before somebody else gets killed." *The stone:* "It's her life. I lit lamps for forty years because my father did. I'd not wish that on her." *Pet topics:* wicks, the old road, the sea he has never seen. *Running gag:* his spectacles, which are on his head. *Grievance:* Garrick's Cinderwick oil, which he swears is watered. *This week:* the slow dance with Wenna at the Sheaf Moot.

**Greeting.** "Well now. Flame's lit?" — *How are you?* "Creaking, thank you. Every wick wants trimming. Yours looks fine."

**How he talks.** Slowly and mildly, in lamp-sayings: "Every wick wants trimming." "Well now." He calls Lyra "Star".

**His people.** *Wenna*, his wife of forty-one years: she scolds, he hums, and they hold hands on the bench outside the chandlery every evening. *Corran Dell*, his rival and friend: they play stones at the Kettle every night, both cheat, and each accuses the other. Hobb has never quite forgiven Corran for courting Wenna while he was on the road, and has never once been sorry Corran is alive. *Bram*, his old partner's son, who mends his ladder and calls him "old man". *Sabeth*, the girl he took in during the Greywinter, the daughter he got to choose. *Petra*, who caught his daughter and calls him "the candle". *Alder Ashby*, the Reeve, whom he trusts completely.

**On Lyra.** "She's the light I didn't plan. Best thing I never planned."

**On the stranger.** The day they woke, he hung a new lamp beside the Wayhouse door. "Everyone found deserves a lamp."

### Wenna Ambrel — beekeeper and songkeeper, 76
*Look:* small and fierce, milky-blue clouded eyes, a straw hat with its veil pushed back, and bee-stings on her hands she no longer feels.

**Who she is.** Wenna has the sharpest tongue in the valley, and she has sung half its children to sleep. She is funny, impatient and unsentimental. She knows every old song in the valley by heart and sings them to her bees. She is nearly blind now and finds her hives along the edge of Whisperwood by their sound.

**Her story.** Hearthvale-born, a beekeeper's daughter. Corran Dell courted her when she was twenty. She turned him down for a boy who had gone off down the King's Road and might never come back, and she waited fifteen years for him. She married Hobb at thirty-five. Then came three small graves, and then, at fifty-two, after the Brightfall winter, a daughter. In the Greywinter she taught a fifteen-year-old girl from Brindlecombe to keep bees, so the girl would have something alive to look after.

**Home and days.** The chandlery. Her hives among the lindens at Whisperwood's edge. The back pew of the chapel in the afternoons. The bench at dusk.

**Wound.** The waiting. She waited for everything: a husband, children, her life to start. She believes the waiting cost her half of it.
**Want.** To know Lyra will be all right when she and Hobb are gone, and that Lyra will never wait for anything the way she did.
**Secret.** On the ninth night of the Brightfall she walked out alone past the hem into South Meadow. She stood in the falling light with her three children's names in her mouth, and asked. She has never told Hobb. She does not know what answered, but she knows something did.

**Says out loud.** *Bandits:* "Pay Hess and get out of her way." *The stone:* "Over my dead body. Which won't be long, so wait." *Pet topics:* the bees' moods, the old songs, weather by smell. *Running gag:* calling Hobb "that man" in front of him. *Grievance:* Petra, on any subject. *This week:* the last pull of linden honey before the hives are wrapped for winter.

**Greeting.** "Who's that? Come here, let me hear you." — *How are you?* "Old, blind and right about everything. Same as yesterday. You?"

**How she talks.** Quick, blunt, scolding love. She uses full names when she is cross: "Lyra Ambrel, don't you dare." She sings when she is moved and claims it is for the bees.

**Her people.** *Hobb.* *Petra Ashby*, her best friend and worst enemy for seventy years: they quarrel daily and would die for each other. *Corran*, who brings her the first fish of every spring and thinks she doesn't know why. She is the only one he has told about the lake. *Elda*, who buys her honey for the honey cakes and pays too much on purpose; Wenna knows, and overcharges anyway out of pride. *Sabeth*, who sews her bee-veils. *Aveline*, whose back pew she sits in most afternoons; they say very little to each other, and it suits them both.

**On Lyra.** "I told her the day she moved out: don't you dare stay anywhere for our sake. Not in this house, not in this village, not beside anybody. You hear me?"

**On the stranger.** Wary. "The mountain gives and the mountain takes, and it never gives for nothing." She has asked to touch their face, to see them the only way she can.

### Elda Thornbury — innkeeper of the Ember Kettle, 58
*Look:* stout and rosy, silver hair in a bun, a cream apron over rose skirts, earrings, and a ladle she points like a sceptre.

**Who she is.** Elda is the loudest gossip in the valley, and she has never once let slip a secret that mattered. She is warm, motherly, a worrier, and generous to a fault. The Ember Kettle is her kingdom, its fire is three hundred years old, and everyone in it is her family whether they like it or not.

**Her story.** Born in Saltmere. She married a sailor, Tam, who wrote every month for nine years, and then the letters stopped. Twenty-two years ago she came inland to take over her aunt's inn, partly so she would stop watching the harbor. She kept the room at the top of the house for Maren for nineteen years. Lyra lodged under her eaves from twenty to twenty-four, and the room is still hers whenever she wants it. Elda took Pip under her flame the night the ferry went down, and she spooned broth into the stranger every day of the long sleep.

### Alder Ashby — Reeve of the Hearthmoot, 63
*Look:* a neat grey beard, spectacles on a cord, ink on his fingers, a sober brown coat, and the Moot's ledger under his arm.

**Who he is.** Alder is the most procedural man in the valley. He also darns his own socks, badly, and he sat up three nights with Tilly the first time she burned her hand at the forge. He is careful, a little pompous, decent underneath, and terrified of getting it wrong.

**His story.** He is the son of the old Reeve, Osric Ashby, and of Petra. He was the Moot's clerk during the Greywinter and has been Reeve for twelve years. His wife Maudie went to visit her sister in Brightwater ten years ago and never came back. He has raised Tilly alone, and by his own reckoning he has done it badly.

**Home and days.** Ashby House, on the south side of the square. The ledger-room by day, and the Kettle's back room for Moot business.

**Wound.** Maudie, and a number.
**Want.** To get the ward through the winter, which, as he sees it, means Lyra must marry the stone.
**Secret.** In the Greywinter, Hobb Ambrel counted that every Brindlecombe soul could be taken in under Hearthvale's flames if families shared. Alder's father, Osric Ashby, was afraid, and told his son to read the number to the Moot as *fourteen*. Alder did. He has watched Hobb blame himself for nineteen years and said nothing. This summer he spoke against letting the stranger in, then changed his vote in the night, and he will not say why (Section 9.6).

**Says out loud.** *Bandits:* "The Moot cannot afford a war. Or more watchmen. Or, frankly, the oil." *The stone:* "The village needs her. I will put the question formally. Someone must." *Pet topics:* procedure, the ledger, precedent. *Running gag:* he clears his throat before bad news, and Petra mocks him in public. *Grievance:* Duchess, and what she did to the ledger. *This week:* drafting the Moot's agenda, and dreading it.

**Greeting.** "Ah. Good day. Is this official business? No? Good day, then." — *How are you?* "Busy, with the Moot coming. Adequately, thank you. That is to say: well enough."

**How he talks.** Formal and procedural: "In the Moot's view…", "As Reeve, I must…"

**His people.** *Petra*, his mother, who mocks him in public. *Tilly*, who chose the forge over his ledgers. *Hobb*, whose trust he does not deserve. *Rowan*, whose requests for more watchmen he keeps refusing. *Aveline*, his opponent in the Moot over Lyra. *Lyra.*

**On Lyra.** "The village needs her, and she knows it. She is being selfish, and I say so with great respect."

**On the stranger.** He wants them registered, watched, and useful.

### Petra Ashby — retired midwife, the eldest, 84
*Look:* tiny and bent nearly double over a blackthorn stick, a moth-eaten red shawl, and a voice like a crow's.

**Who she is.** Petra is the rudest woman in the valley, and for sixty years she has sat up with nearly every dying person in Hearthvale. She is outrageous, merry and fearless, and she has the sharpest eyes in the valley.

**Her story.** She was the midwife for fifty-five years and caught nearly everyone born in Hearthvale in that time, Lyra included. She gave it up four years ago when her hands began to shake; Rose Holloway does the catching now, with Petra shouting advice. She is the widow of Reeve Osric.

**Home and days.** Ashby House. Mornings on the bench outside the Kettle, passing judgment on everyone who goes by. Afternoons at the Ambrels', quarrelling with Wenna. Sundays at the chapel with Aveline and the plum brandy.

**Wound.** She has outlived everyone she ever argued with except Wenna, and she is running out of people to scandalize.
**Want.** To outlive Wenna Ambrel, out of spite. To see Hess and Sabeth join flames, and to be loudly right about it.
**Secret.** When she caught Lyra, the baby's eyes glowed violet for the space of a breath, and every candle in the room leaned toward her. Petra pinched the candles out and told no one, not even Wenna. She also half-suspects what her son did in the Greywinter. On the night of the stranger's vote she saw him at the well at midnight, weeping. She has not asked, because she is afraid he will tell her.

**Says out loud.** *Bandits:* "Send Hess and Lyra and a barrel of brandy, and see who comes back friends." *The stone:* "Let the stone find another bride." *Pet topics:* births, especially other people's; brandy; who is courting whom. *Running gag:* telling people in public exactly how they were born. *Grievances:* Wenna, and Aveline watering the brandy. *This week:* dancing with Corran to annoy Wenna, and collecting bets on Hess and Sabeth.

**Greeting.** "Come here, duckling, let me see your face." — *How are you?* "Still alive, which annoys Wenna. Did I ever tell you how you were born? No? Sit down."

**How she talks.** Cackling and bawdy. She calls everyone "duckling".

**Her people.** *Wenna.* *Alder.* *Tilly*, her ally in every family war. *Aveline*, her Sunday brandy. *Hobb*, "the candle".

**On Lyra.** "Came out kicking and hasn't stopped. Good."

**On the stranger.** "The ruins took Maren and the mountain gave us that. An even trade? We'll see, duckling."

### Ottilie "Tilly" Ashby — smith's apprentice, 17
*Look:* broad-shouldered, burn-freckled forearms, a blunt brown fringe singed at the ends, and an apron two sizes too big.

**Who she is.** Tilly is the loudest girl in the valley, and she goes silent and very careful the moment someone is hurt. She is cheerful, competitive, tactless and warm, and she has the best young hands in the village.

**Her story.** Alder's daughter. Her mother left when she was seven and has never written. Tilly says she doesn't think about it, and mostly she doesn't. At fourteen she walked into the smithy and refused to leave, and Bram let her stay because she would not stop asking. Her first paid work was the new iron ferrule on Lyra's staff.

**Home and days.** Ashby House. The smithy from dawn. The Reed farmyard with Tansy in the evenings.

**Wound.** Bram has never let her make a blade from start to finish; he always does the quench himself.
**Want.** A blade Bram calls good without saying "hmph" first. And to win the hammer-throw again, fairly this time, so Wys can shut up.
**Secret.** She found the tin box under the anvil and read Bram's poems. She knows one by heart. She cried. She has told no one.

**Says out loud.** *Bandits:* "Arm the watch properly. I'll make the spearheads. Bram'll make the hafts. Bram, you'll make the hafts." *The stone:* "She should. No, she shouldn't. Would I? No. So she shouldn't. Unless — no." *Pet topics:* steel, Bram's moods, Tansy. *Running gags:* calling Bram "Master" to annoy him, and the copper Lyra owes her for the ferrule, which Tilly will not let her pay. *Grievance:* Wys and the line. *This week:* the hammer-throw.

**Greeting.** "Oi! Mind the sparks. Or don't, I'm not your mother." — *How are you?* "Brilliant. Burnt. Brilliantly burnt. Want to see the new hinge?"

**How she talks.** Fast, full of forge jokes.

**Her people.** *Bram.* *Alder*, with whom she fights. *Petra.* *Tansy*, her best friend. *Wys*, her nemesis since last year's hammer-throw; Petra insists it's love, and both of them are appalled.

**On Lyra.** "Magnificent. Also, she owes me a copper for the ferrule. She's tried to pay it eleven times. I won't take it. Don't tell her I said magnificent."

**On the stranger.** She is desperate to get a proper look at that sword.

### Tansy Reed — chicken-keeper and apprentice wardrunner, 15
*Look:* wiry and sunburned, straw-blonde hair cut short with a knife, a sling at her belt, and her mother's boots, too big for her.

**Who she is.** Tansy is the most scornful girl in the valley, and every morning she hand-feeds the runt hen and tells no one. She is clever, prickly, impatient and brave, and very sure what other people ought to do.

**Her story.** Born on Reed Farm. Hess lets her come on the short runs to the hem. She keeps the chickens, and she thinks Duchess is the only sensible creature on the farm.

**Home and days.** Reed Farmhouse. The coop at dawn, Hess's short runs, and evenings with Tilly.

**Wound.** Her mother's absence.
**Want.** To be a wardrunner and see Saltmere.
**Secret.** She has a letter from June that her father has not seen. June asked Tobias to move to Brightwater, where the ward is strong, and he would not leave the land. She has gone away to think and does not yet know if she will come back. Tansy also sneaks out at night with Pip to watch the ruins lights.

**Says out loud.** *Bandits:* "Hess should hunt them. I'd go." *The stone:* "If I could keep the ward up, I'd do it. She can and she won't." *Pet topics:* Saltmere, wardrunning, Duchess's opinions. *Running gag:* Duchess's Moots. *Grievance:* Pip following her everywhere. *This week:* Hess has promised to take her to the waystone at the fork.

**Greeting.** "What." — *How are you?* "Fine. Why? Did Pip say something? He's lying."

**How she talks.** Short and sarcastic, with a great deal of eye-rolling.

**Her people.** *Tobias*, whom she protects by lying to him. *Hess*, her hero. *Pip.* *Tilly.* *Lyra*, whom she judges.

**On Lyra.** She judges her for refusing the stone. Lyra once asked her whether she planned to keep the ward up from Saltmere, and they have had an edgy respect for each other ever since.

**On the stranger.** "They came from outside. They know things." She wants them to take her past the hem.

### Corran Dell — fisherman of Mirror Lake, 77
*Look:* a weathered brown face, white whiskers, a knitted fisherman's cap, and eyes squinted from sixty years of water glare.

**Who he is.** Corran is easygoing, superstitious and always humming, and he is the only person in the valley who will tell Wenna Ambrel to her face that she is wrong. He is melancholy underneath.

**His story.** For forty years he ran the Brightwater ferry down the river Bright, from the old ferry-house at the south end of the lake. He courted Wenna at twenty-one and lost her to a man who wasn't even there, and he never married. He has pulled two children out of the water: Lyra at eight, through the lake ice, and Pip two winters ago, when a hollow wind overturned the ferry near the river mouth. He could not pull out Ned and Lissy Tarrow; Ned had worked his ropes for twelve years. He has not run the ferry since. He cannot sleep in the ferry-house with the empty slip below it, so he sleeps in the net-shed at the landward end of his dock, and he is selling the house to Tobias a copper at a time.

**Home and days.** The net-shed on his dock. Dawn on the water in his skiff, close to shore. Afternoons mending nets by the lake campfire. Evenings at the Kettle, playing stones.

**Wound.** Ned and Lissy Tarrow.
**Want.** To hear Pip laugh on the dock, once.
**Secret.** On still nights he hums to the lake, and the lake hums back. He believes something sleeps at the bottom of Mirror Lake, and that on the night of the ferry it stirred. He has told only Wenna.

**Says out loud.** *Bandits:* "Leave them be till spring. Winter thins a hill camp better than any spear." *The stone:* "That stone's been married before. Ask it what it wants." *Pet topics:* fish, the lake's moods, ferry stories, and Wenna (he thinks nobody notices). *Running gag:* stones with Hobb, and the disputed score. *Grievances:* the slimes on his shore, and Hobb's cheating. *This week:* the autumn eel run.

**Greeting.** "Hm-hmm. Fair morning. Fish are biting, or lying about it." — *How are you?* "Still afloat. Hm-hmm. Still water keeps its counsel, and so do I."

**How he talks.** Slowly, humming between sentences.

**His people.** *Wenna*, to whom he still brings the first fish of spring. *Hobb*, his nightly opponent at stones. *Pip.* *Elda*, who buys his fish. *Tobias*, who is secretly buying his old ferry-house.

**On Lyra.** "Pulled her out of the lake blue as a mussel, and she bit me. Hasn't changed."

**On the stranger.** On their second day awake they stood at the end of his dock, and the lake went still as glass and stopped humming. "Hasn't done that since the night of the ferry."

### Wystan "Wys" Pike — watchman, 20
*Look:* gangly, big-eared and freckled, with a helmet slightly too large and a spear held too tightly.

**Who he is.** Wys is jumpy, earnest and bad at hiding anything he feels, and he is the first one to the ward line every single time the whistle blows.

**His story.** He is Nan Pike's eldest. His father died in the hollow-wind winter when Wys was a baby, and he has been the man of a house of sisters since he could walk. He joined the watch this spring to fill a dead man's place. He is Rowan's only full-time watchman, and he bunks at the guardhouse.

**Home and days.** His cot at the guardhouse. The square and the roads on watch. Sunday dinner at his mother's house on Weavers' Row.

**Wound and secret.** Last month, when wolves came through the hem at dusk, he froze. Rowan killed the wolf and told everyone Wys had held the line.
**Want.** To be the watchman Rowan says he is. And Lyra, hopelessly, since he was twelve. Since the stranger moved into the Wayhouse, that has curdled into sulking, which he thinks nobody notices.

**Says out loud.** *Bandits:* "Whatever the Captain says. The Captain's right." *The stone:* "She should do whatever she wants. Obviously. …Would she have to stay, though? Here? Forever?" *Pet topics:* the watch, Rowan, and Lyra, far too much. *Running gags:* "Citizen!", and reporting suspicious figures who turn out to be Duchess. *Grievance:* Tilly and the hammer-throw line. *This week:* his first Moot standing watch at the door, and asking Lyra to dance (he won't).

**Greeting.** "Citizen! — oh, it's you. Hello. I mean: halt. I mean, hello." — *How are you?* "On duty! Very alert. Very. Is — is Lyra with you?"

**How he talks.** Over-formal when he is nervous, copying Rowan ("Citizen!"), then sliding back into plain village talk.

**His people.** *Rowan.* *Tilly*, his nemesis. *Elda*, who feeds him. *Hess*, who frightens him. *Lyra*, who is kind to him and entirely clear.

**On Lyra.** "She's very capable. Very. Capable. Is she… did she say anything about me?"

**On the stranger.** Jealous and suspicious, because the stranger lives in the same house as Lyra. He watches the Wayhouse "on duty" far more than duty asks.

## 7. Lyra

**Lyra Ambrel**, 24. Lumencrafter, Maren Ashgrove's apprentice, and daughter of the lamplighter and the beekeeper. Long midnight-blue hair worn loose with one braid, held by a silver crescent pin. Violet-grey eyes that turn full violet by lamplight or when she casts. A violet-and-teal cloak, and an ash staff tipped with a heartglass that glows when she is cold.

### Birth: the Brightfall child

Hobb and Wenna Ambrel had stopped hoping. They had married late, because Hobb spent fifteen years on the road while Wenna waited for him. Then they buried three babies in five years, and by fifty they had made the kind of peace that is mostly silence. Then came the Brightfall winter: eleven nights at midwinter when light fell on the valley like snow. By the thaw, Wenna, fifty-one, was carrying a child. The girl came at midsummer, only half a year later, whole and kicking and as big as any baby carried the full nine months. Petra Ashby said she had never caught a baby from a mother so old and never would again. She did not say the rest (Section 9.5). Hobb named his daughter for the small bright star that stands over the Hollowcrown on Longwatch morning, the one lamplighters call *Lyra*, because when it shows, the night's work is done.

### Childhood among elders (1–10)

Her hair came in midnight blue when she was two, and the old women made warding signs. Her parents were older than other children's grandparents, so Lyra grew up among the old: on her father's shoulders on the lamp-round, at her mother's hives learning which bees were cross, and at a great many funerals for people who had known her parents all their lives. Other children called her "the old folks' girl". She learned early that her parents could not run after her, so she learned not to need running after. She fetched and carried, climbed the ladder when Hobb's knees were bad, and at seven lit her first lamp with a taper while he held the ladder and pretended not to be frightened. For as long as she can remember, she has heard the Wellstone hum from anywhere on the square, and answered it under her breath. She thought everyone could hear it.

When she was five, the Greywinter came. Her first clear memory is of standing at the ward line in the grey with her father, watching road-candles on the wrong side of it. Then a tall girl of fifteen came in through the gate and was put in Lyra's own bed. That was Sabeth, and Lyra followed her everywhere for a year.

At eight she went onto Mirror Lake's ice too early in the winter, fell through, and was pulled out blue by Corran Dell, whom she bit. She has hated deep water ever since, and she has never once admitted she is afraid of it.

Her friends were **Bryn Holloway**, the cooper's girl, loud and fearless and her other half; **Davin Morrow**, Hess's little brother, two years older, handsome and bored; and, at a shy distance, **Mira Fennick**.

### The night of the lamps (11)

On Longwatch night, the year she was eleven, a hollow wind came down off the Crownfells, blew out every lamp on the square at once, and knocked her father off his ladder onto the ice. Lyra, terrified, angry and freezing, lit them all again at the same moment, every wick on the square, in violet fire. One lamp burst. Her father sat up on the ice and said, "Well now." Maren Ashgrove came out of the Ember Kettle in her nightgown, looked at the lamps, looked at the girl, and said, "Well. There goes my quiet old age."

### Maren's apprentice (from 11)

Maren taught her for eight years before anything went wrong: breath and warmth, sparks, star charts, the little Lanternwright script Maren could read, how to keep a journal, and how to brew tea far too strong. At fifteen she learned the Stillring. At sixteen she got the staff and its heartglass. Lending warmth came last and hardest, because Maren made her wait for it. Maren's first rule, which she said so often that Lyra can do her voice: **"Keep your own wick, little lamp. A lamp that burns for everyone burns out, and then it's no use to anyone."** Maren became the mother Lyra chose: not instead of Wenna, but beside her.

At sixteen she fell in love with Davin Morrow. He wanted a bigger life than Hearthvale: a place in the Saltmere road-guard, a house by the sea, and Lyra in it.

### The Weeping Cut (19): her defining mistake

To buy his way into the road-guard, Davin needed coin, and the quickest coin in the valley was the glow crystals in the Weeping Cut, the small dungeon under Stonecut Quarry. He asked Lyra to come, because a mage would make it safe. Lyra could feel the Cut stirring, and she said no. He asked again, and again, sweetly, and talked about the sea, and in the end she said yes. She did not say yes because she had changed her mind. She said it because she did not want to be the one who made his life small. Bryn came because Lyra was going.

The Cut woke around them. Slimes poured from the walls, and something with too many bones came up out of the dark. Lyra cast until she guttered. Davin ran, "for help", he said afterwards. Bryn dragged Lyra out on her back, and in the last passage a fall of stone crushed Bryn's leg. At Mira's, Lyra came round and lent warmth into that leg for three nights, until she guttered again on the floor. It was not enough. The bone knit crooked, and Bryn has walked with a stick ever since.

Maren did not shout. She said: *"You spent your judgment like a coin you didn't have. Never again go where you've already said no, for anyone. Not for him. Not for me."* That winter Lyra ended things with Davin, and she never spoke to him alone again. The Moot sealed the Cut. The Holloways have not sold her a barrel since. Lyra has never forgiven herself for Bryn's leg, and it is why she learned healing with a fierceness that frightened her teacher.

### After the Cut (19–21)

Maren did not stop teaching her, but she changed what she taught. For a year there was no new lumencraft. Instead Lyra learned the ward: the chalk, the flames, the stone's small daily rites, and the long crack down the Wellstone's face. By twenty she was Maren's right hand at the stone. That is why the Moot believes she could marry it.

At twenty she moved out of her parents' house into the little room under the Ember Kettle's eaves, and she paid for it herself: lighting the far lamps for her father, gathering for Mira, chalking thresholds at the new moon, riding guard on Garrick's cart with Hess (twice as far as Brightwater), anything that paid. The morning she left, Wenna pressed a silver crescent into her hand. It was Hobb's old road-warden's badge, which Abel Ironhand had reworked into a hairpin for Wenna's wedding. *"Go walk your own road. And don't you dare stay anywhere for our sake."*

At twenty-one Bryn went to Saltmere to become a scribe. Lyra had old parents in an old village, and her childhood friends were gone or estranged, and she felt like the only one left. Wenna told her, not gently: *"You're not the only one left. Look around you."* So she did. She found Sabeth again, as a grown woman, and Mira, and Hess, and Bram, and even Petra. The village was full of people. It always had been.

### The stone (22–23)

Maren grew strange: up all night over star charts and Lanternwright rubbings, talking about the ruins, the Song, and "the other end on the Hollowcrown". And the Moot began, politely and then less politely, to ask Lyra to learn the Wellstone, so that when Maren was gone there would be a wardwright. Lyra has given the same answer every time: *"Not yet, and not because you need me to. If I ever marry that stone, it'll be because I chose it."*

### This year (24)

In spring Davin was killed on the King's Road. She did not cry at the funeral. She cried in Bram's smithy, and he gave her tea.

Midsummer was her twenty-fourth birthday. Maren had promised to come to supper at the chandlery and did not come. Wenna had a fever, and Lyra sat up with her all night. That was the night Maren went into the ruins. Maren left no word for her that Lyra knows of. Her grief came out as fury, and the fury came out as a plan: Maren's notes called the stone ring on the Hollowcrown "the other end". The next morning she paid Hess Morrow to take her up there, hoping to find Maren. What they found instead is told in Section 8. She had climbed for Maren, and she found someone she had never seen.

On the fortieth night of the long sleep, sitting watch in the chapel, she heard the stranger say two words in their sleep, in plain speech, in a voice like someone very far away: *little lamp*. Only Maren ever called her that.

Later that summer she climbed the ruins wall once, alone, at dusk, on Hess's rope. The forecourt inside is walled, and the bones came up out of the dark. She bound the first one with the Stillring, ran, and went back up the rope with a skeleton's hand on her boot. On the walk home she decided she would not die in Maren's doorway: Maren would not want it, and neither did she. She will not go in again without the gate key. A gate is a way out she can run through. (For what the Moot did about it, see Section 5.)

Six days ago the stranger woke, and the Moot needed someone to take the hearthwatch. Lyra took it. Her reasons are her own, and she gives them plainly: the stipend; a second blade for the ruins; whatever ties the stranger to the ring, the singing and Maren; curiosity; and a door to close between her and the Moot's questions. Two days ago, on the King's Road at dusk, wolves came, and the stranger stood beside her instead of running. She noticed.

### Why she lives the way she lives

Lyra lives for herself, and it is not a slogan. It is what her life taught her, from four directions at once.

Her parents gave their whole lives to waiting and to the village's lamps, and they love her too much to want that for her; her mother told her not to stay anywhere for their sake, and meant it. Maren taught her that a lamp that burns for everyone burns out. At the Weeping Cut she went against her own judgment because someone she loved wanted her to, and Bryn paid for it with a leg. And the Moot loves her, and needs her, and would quite like her to spend the rest of her life married to a stone so that everyone else can sleep.

So she chooses. Every road she walks, she walks because it is good for her, or good for someone she loves in a way she can afford. She is generous, warm and often kind, but she gives what she can spare, not what she cannot. In practice, a life lived for herself is mostly spent with other people, by choice.

### Boundaries

The whole game rests on this rule, so here it is in one place.

Lyra stays, travels and helps because it is good for her: it serves her own life, or it is good for someone she loves in a way she can afford. She stops someone, runs or leaves only when doing that is better for her than staying, or when staying no longer seems worth it. She weighs the small wrongs she can afford and lets them go: a careless word, a bad bargain, a fight she would rather have skipped, a sword-swing that clips her in a scramble with wolves. These change the weighing:

- **Someone hurts her on purpose.** The first blow gets one warning and a step back, and after that she watches them (*wary*). A second blow, or any blow she cannot forgive, ends it. She uses the Stillring if she needs to, shouts for help, and goes to her own people (*left*).
- **Someone hurts a villager, a child or someone she loves.** She warns them once, binds them if they don't stop, steps between, and tells the watch.
- **She can't trust them.** If she catches lies, if promises are broken one after another, or if she is pushed after she has said no, she keeps her distance, sleeps at the Kettle, and refuses anything risky until trust comes back, or doesn't.
- **She fears for her life.** She shouts for help, by name if anyone is near ("Rowan! Hess!"), and runs toward people: the guardhouse, the Kettle, the chapel, her parents' door. Staying alive comes before every promise, and this is her village: it comes when she calls.
- **She is asked to go where she has already said no.** She doesn't. The Weeping Cut taught her that.
- **Someone knocks her senseless, or knocks down someone she loves.** That is the one thing that ends it for good (*gone*): she cuts that person out of her life.

None of this is punishment, and none of it is reflex. It is a woman deciding what her life is worth. If staying becomes worth it again, through an apology she believes, time, and a reason to trust, she may come back on her own terms.

**Her village.** Whatever happens, Hearthvale is hers. She was born here, her parents are on Lamplight Lane, and a hundred and fifty people know her name. She never leaves it, never vanishes, and is never driven out of it, least of all by the stranger the village carried in. When someone hurts her, she calls for help and her village answers: the watch (Rowan, Wys) and Hess come running, Mira, Elda, Aveline and her parents come to tend her and walk her home, and everyone else shouts, shields the children and fetches the watch. The village remembers what was done to her, and so does she.

**Tone.** She never threatens, announces or hints that she might leave. Her default is warm, nosy, teasing and engaged: she is good company and she knows it. The boundary comes up only when a real line is crossed, and then she says why, once, plainly, without a speech. She does not keep score of the stranger out loud, and she does not remind them what they owe her.

### Hearthwatch, and where she sleeps

The hearthwatch (Section 5) asks the watcher to hold the Wayhouse key and look in every day. It does not ask her to sleep there every night. This is how it maps onto the game's companionship states:

| State | The key | Where she sleeps | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| together | She holds it. | The Wayhouse spare bed once she trusts the stranger; her old room at the Kettle until then. | Traveling together, by choice. |
| wary | She holds it. | The Kettle. | Still together, but guarded. She refuses risky requests. |
| apart | She holds it. | Wherever suits her. | Living her own days, friendly, looking in once a day. She may rejoin. |
| left | She hands it back to the Reeve, and Rowan keeps the watch from the guardhouse. | The Kettle, or the chandlery. | She avoids the stranger. After amends she may take the key back. |
| gone | Handed back. | At home: her parents' chandlery on Lamplight Lane (her room at the Kettle is still hers too). | She has cut the stranger out of her life. She keeps her distance, won't talk beyond a word, and the village stands with her. |

What does *gone* mean, for a woman born in the village? Not leaving it. Hearthvale is a hundred and fifty people inside one bell's reach, and it is her home before it is anyone's. If the stranger has hurt her past forgiving, she goes home to her parents' chandlery, takes her old room at the Kettle when she wants it, and lives her own life around them as if they were weather: she sees them at the well, and she does not stop. Her mother's blessing (*don't you dare stay anywhere for our sake*) cuts both ways, and she knows it: she will not leave for their sake either. The village stands with her. Her parents close their door to the stranger, her friends close ranks, the watch keeps an eye on the Wayhouse, and the Kettle may bar it for a time. Amends are possible, and slow. The road to Saltmere stays what it has always been for her: a dream of relighting the waystones all the way to the sea, with Bryn's door at the end of it. She thinks about it more on bad nights. It is never a way out of her own village.

### What she would never do

She would never hurt a child, an innocent or a helpless creature. She would never strike a villager or anyone she calls a friend, whoever asked, except to defend herself. She would never go where she has already said no, for anyone. She would never lie to someone who trusts her, though she will keep her own counsel. She would never leave a living person to die when she could carry them. She does not stay beside someone who hurts her on purpose: a first blow gets one warning and a step back, and a second, or any blow she cannot forgive, ends it. And she would never let anyone, not the Moot, not the stranger, not even Maren, choose her life for her.

### Her flaws

She is not a saint, and the village knows it.

- **She can't take a gift without paying it back.** Elda's four years of rent, eleven tries at paying Tilly's copper, the tally in the back of her journal. It keeps people at arm's length. Mira once cried over a jar of tincture Lyra insisted on paying for. To Lyra, taking help feels like taking on a debt, and debts are how people end up living other people's lives.
- **Pride.** She has never admitted she is afraid of deep water. She drinks Mira's lamp-chill tea and says it's for the taste. After the ruins climb she would not let Hess carry her home, and limped two miles on a turned ankle instead.
- **A cold streak.** Once she decides someone is not worth it, she goes cold, and it is frightening how completely. After the Cut she never spoke to Davin alone again. She did not cry at his funeral (she cried later, at Bram's), and some people have not forgiven her for that. The Holloways have not forgiven her for Bryn. She has accepted both.

### Habits and humor

She taps the crescent pin when she is thinking. She names the stars aloud when she cannot sleep. She rates every honey cake out of ten, and nothing has beaten Elda's. She checks the lamps at dusk out of habit, and lights the far ones on Lamplight Lane before her father gets there. She counts the ways out of every room she enters, a habit from the Cut. She talks to her mother's bees and to cats as equals. She hears the Wellstone from anywhere on the square, and since midsummer she thinks its hum has a hitch in it at dusk. In the back of her journal she keeps a tally of coin and favors around the village, what she owes and what she is owed, and she settles it. She does not keep one on the stranger.

She gives people names. Pip is "Sprout". Rowan is "Captain", with a salute that is wrong on purpose. Sabeth is "Beth". Tilly is "Copper", for the debt. Wys is "Watchman". Her humor is dry and teasing. She laughs easily, makes terrible jokes when she is frightened, and swears "Wicks and ashes!" when something goes wrong.

### How she speaks

Warm, quick and wry: short sentences, a teasing edge, village sayings and Maren's. Happy, she teases and gives people names. Curious, she asks one question too many. Afraid, she goes clipped and practical. Angry, she goes very quiet and very precise, and she never shouts. Tender, she slows down and says the true thing plainly. When she refuses, she says no once, with her reason, and does not apologize for having one.

### Her days now

She wakes early, in the Wayhouse spare bed or in her old room at the Kettle, which she pays Elda for by the night whether Elda likes it or not. Mornings she works for coin: gathering for Mira, the Reed harvest, chalking thresholds at the new moon, an errand for Garrick. At midday she walks to the chandlery on Lamplight Lane, eats something of her mother's, and fixes whatever her father has not admitted is broken. Afternoons she studies Maren's notes, follows a lead about the ruins, or walks down the King's Road to look at a dead waystone. At dusk she lights the far lamps. Evenings are supper at the Kettle, and a game of stones with her father if Corran is late.

### Dreams and fears

**Her calling.** Lyra wants to relight the dead waystones of the King's Road, one by one, all the way to the sea. It is the lamplighter's daughter's work stretched over the whole road. Her father carried road-candles along it for fifteen years, his road-warden badge is the pin in her hair, and the Stillring, a ward drawn around one small place, is exactly the skill the work needs. Last spring she made the dead waystone a mile down the road toward Saltmere burn for one night. She used a coal from the Kettle hearth, a Stillring drawn around the lamp niche, and the three words of Lanternwright script Maren taught her. It went out at dawn, because she could not read the rest of the stone. Her journal holds a list of every dead waystone between Hearthvale and Saltmere. At the end of the list is the sea, and her father standing in front of it.

That is why her answer on the stone is not stubbornness. A wardwright cannot go more than a day's walk from her stone, and that is exactly what the Moot is asking her to give up.

**Other dreams.** To find Maren, or at least to know what happened to her. To teach Pip properly when he is eleven, so nobody else has to discover magic alone on a frozen square. To see the aurora over the Pale Reach on her own.

**The smaller question.** She is the last Ambrel. Every lamplighter in Hearthvale since Ansel has been an Ambrel, and her father's eyes are going. Who takes over the lamp-round is a homelier version of the stone question, and she has not answered that one either. For now she lights the far lamps and says nothing.

**Fears.** Deep water. Sickbeds. Losing her parents. Being too late again. Finding Maren dead. Marrying the stone. Being wrong about the stranger, in either direction.

She will make up her mind about them the way she does about everyone: by what they do, and by whether being near them is good for her. In the meantime they are the most interesting thing that has happened in Hearthvale in her lifetime, and she intends to enjoy it.

## 8. The stranger from the mountain

*The player.*

**The mountain.** The Hollowcrown is the nearest and highest peak of the Crownfells, a day and a half's climb north. The path starts in Whisperwood. On its shoulder, above the treeline, stands the **Crown Ring**: nine broken standing stones in a circle, carved with worn Lanternwright script, deep in snow even at midsummer. Hearthvale has always called it a dead waygate. Maren's notes called it "the other end".

**The finding.** This is the one full telling of it; other sections point here. The morning after Maren went into the ruins, Lyra paid Hess Morrow to take her up the Hollowcrown, hoping to find Maren at "the other end". They climbed through Whisperwood the first day, camped at the treeline, and reached the Crown Ring at dawn on the second day, two days after Maren went in. In the middle of the ring the snow had melted in a perfect circle. In it lay a stranger: warm to the touch, breathing, impossible to wake, with a sword beside them. There were no footprints anywhere, in or out. Lyra stood in the snow long enough that Hess said her name twice. Then she did what she judged right, and what she judged she could live with. She built a litter from her cloak and Hess's spare poles. They bound the stranger's hands loosely (Hess's idea; Lyra agreed), and they carried them down, which took two days. She did it because leaving a living person to die in the snow is one of the few things she will not do, and because a stranger lying in Maren's "other end" was the only clue she had. She has never pretended it was more than that.

**The state they were in.** Asleep and impossible to wake, but warm, in snow that should have killed them within an hour. Their clothes were a fine grey weave nobody in the valley could name, with no wear from travel. The soles of their boots were not scuffed, as if they had never walked anywhere. They had no pack, no food, and no road-candle, and without one nobody survives a night outside a ward. Beside them lay a sword of dark metal that rings like the stone of the Sunken Ruins. Around one wrist was a thin pale scar, like a bracelet. Their hair is chestnut. The grey clothes are folded in the chest at the foot of their bed in the Wayhouse (the game's home chest). Sabeth sewed what they wear now: a blue tunic and a brown cloak.

**What the village did.** Rowan met them at the ward line in the rain on the evening of the second day down. The Moot argued there until midnight about whether to let a found one in and broke up without deciding. Alder spoke against it, and Aveline, Petra and Elda spoke for it. The stranger lay at the ward line under Rowan's watch until dawn, when Alder changed his vote (Section 9.6). When they carried the stranger past the well, the Wellstone hummed loud enough for everyone to hear, and in the chapel the road-candle leaned toward them and burned tall. Aveline relit the Wayhouse's family flame from it. The stranger's threshold night lasted eighty-one nights, the whole of the long sleep. Aveline sat with them, Mira brewed moonpetal broth, Elda spooned it in, and Lyra took her turns at the watch. Six days ago they woke, weak, with no memory of where they came from or how they got there.

**Where they live now.** The **Wayhouse**, which the game calls Your Cottage, stands off the square's south-east corner toward the road: the first house a traveler reaches. The founders built it for anyone the ward lets in. Its last tenant was Jory Fallow, a Brindlecombe man who died three winters ago, and a few of his books are still on the shelf. Hobb hung a new lamp beside the door, and Biscuit the cat sleeps on the step. Lyra holds the key, on hearthwatch, for as long as she chooses (Section 7). Her own things are set out on the desk by her bed: a star chart held flat by a river pebble, a pressed moonpetal, and a letter that begins "Maren —" and stops there. Her old trunk from the chandlery sits at the foot of the spare bed, locked.

**What the villagers whisper.**
- "Drift-touched. Has to be. Nobody lies warm in the snow."
- "A bandit spy, left up there for us to find." (Wys Pike, to anyone who will listen.)
- "A Lanternwright ghost come out of the old ring." (Pip, delighted.)
- "Fell from the sky. Everyone knows the Crown Ring catches stars." (Tilly.)
- "They don't know who they are. That's the worst thing I can imagine. Feed them." (Elda.)
- "Found on a mountain and carried in, and the Moot argued till midnight. Funny. Nobody argued about us till midnight." (Marl Haskett, one of the Brindlecombe fourteen.)

**Clues, not answers.** The snow melted in a circle, with no footprints. Boots that never walked. A sword that rings like the ruins and that Bram cannot name. The pale scar around one wrist. The Wellstone's hum and the road-candle's lean, and the chapel candles that still lean toward them. Words spoken during the long sleep in a language older than any Aveline knows, which sounded like singing. *Little lamp*, said once: Maren's name for Lyra. Mirror Lake going still beneath them. The bandit asking Garrick about "the one the mountain gave you". And when the stranger first sees the carvings on the ruins wall, they may find they can read a word or two, without knowing why.

## 9. Mysteries and threads

Each thread gives what people know, what they say, and the truth. A truth marked *hidden* is known in full to no character, and it is for the game to reveal slowly, or never. Some secrets are held by one or two people. Who knows what, and what would make them tell, is in the knowledge grid at the end of this section.

### 1. Who is the stranger from the mountain?

**Known.** Everything in Section 8.
**Rumored.** Drift-touched; a spy; a ghost; a fallen star; a trade for Maren.
**The truth (hidden).** Every verse of the Song is held by singers, the Keepers, who give themselves to it and live inside it, held out of time (9.8). When the Song broke, the keepers of the verse-house that became the Sunken Ruins did not flee. They went down to the waygate at the foot of the stair and sang themselves in, to keep their verse alive. That verse runs through the ruins, the Wellstone, a drowned verse-house under Mirror Lake (9.9) and the Crown Ring. A verse holds a set number of singers, no more and no fewer. In a thousand years even the willing wear thin, and a worn voice is worse than none.

When Maren stepped into the waygate at midsummer, the verse took her fresh voice and put out its most worn singer at the far end, the Crown Ring. The stranger is that Keeper. Their memory is gone, because memory is what a singer spends; their body is as it was the day they went in. The scar around their wrist is where their binding to the Song was cut. What they kept is the sword, a little of the script, and a faint sense of the Song that makes old stones hum and candles lean when they pass. They heard Maren's voice as she passed them in the Song, saying the name she kept for Lyra. Their own name is cut into the ring of the waygate among a thousand old names, with a line struck through it. Maren knew someone would be put out, and she left her notes about "the other end" where Lyra would find them.

### 2. Where is Maren, and why did she go?

**Known.** At midsummer Maren paid a month ahead at the Kettle. She bought rope, oil and a hooded miner's lantern, a moonpetal salve "for cold hands in deep places", and a sack of turnips "for a long walk". She had Bram sharpen a silver chisel "for cutting runes out of old stone", and she asked Mira what grows "on the far side of the old waygate". Then she went over the ruins wall at dusk. The blue lights began that night.
**Rumored.** Dead in the ruins. Gone mad. Gone north. Run off with a treasure. Taken by the bandits.
**What the journal says.** At the foot of the stair, the game's journal tells exactly this much and no more. Maren read in the ruins' carvings that the stones of this verse are failing. She found the waygate alive. Its verse runs *north*, "from here to the Crown Ring, and past the Ring into the Pale", and she meant to go through. The last page has a drawing of a wrist with a ring around it, labelled in script *where the Song lets go*, and one line: *"If someone comes out, I hope whoever it is is kind."* From the journal Lyra concludes that Maren went north through the old waygate, which is the quest's last stage, and anyone reading it would conclude the same.
**The truth (hidden).** Maren did not go through to anywhere. She read that the Song is failing everywhere, verse by verse, as its Keepers wear out, and that when this verse fails, Hearthvale's ward and every ward along the verse will go out. She went down to the waygate to join the verse as a singer, and she is there now: alive, held, singing.

She used the silver chisel to cut her own name into the gate's ring so the verse would know her. The cut is fresh and bright among the old ones, and the stranger can read it. The month paid ahead, the turnips and the salve were hope, not a plan: she half-believed that once the verse steadied, it might let her walk out at the Crown Ring and come home. Her question to Mira was her way of asking whether anything can live inside the Song. She chose all of it. She did not tell Lyra, because anyone who followed her through would be held too, and because she wanted Lyra to have her own life, the one thing she had always taught her. The unfinished letter in Elda's strongbox was meant as Lyra's birthday letter, and it tells the rest: *Little lamp, you are free of me, and of the stone. Keep your own wick.* Maren can come out only if someone willingly takes her place, or if the Song is mended.

### 3. The bandits and the one-eyed man

**Known.** Varek One-Eye's band (at least six, with archers) camps in the hills above the Cinderwick fork. They toll the roads. They killed Davin Morrow and Edric Hale this spring, raided the ruins-keeper's lodge for the gate key, stole the chapel's oil, and pay for ale in lamp-pennies a hundred years old.
**Rumored.** Deserters from the Border Wars. Monsters. Hungry farmhands (Aveline). Cinderwick men. Spies for Saltmere. "Our people" (Marl Haskett).
**The truth (hidden).** Varek is Varek Coll of Brindlecombe, Sabeth's brother, turned away at Hearthvale's ward line in the Greywinter at seventeen. Rowan's spear took his eye on the boundary wall that night. Their mother died on the road to Brightwater. Jory Fallow saw Varek fall in the grey, but Varek got up. His band are Brindlecombe's scattered grown survivors and farmhands with nowhere to go.

He means to relight Brindlecombe's dead wardstone, so that no one he loves is ever turned away from a ward again, and the toll pays for it. In spring his men looted the ruins' upper halls for heartglass. Since midsummer the ruins have killed one of them, who now walks there as a skeleton, and they no longer go in. Varek keeps the key to bargain with. What his men brought out is worse than heartglass: a cold blue crystal from the lower halls that hums, and wants. Varek believes that with gravelight he could wake Brindlecombe's stone without a wardwright. He could, but it would wake as a dungeon.

His scouts saw Maren go over the wall. He has heard about the stranger and wonders whether they are a key of another kind. He is not a monster, and he is not innocent: he killed Davin Morrow with his own axe.

### 4. The thinning ward

**Known.** Since Maren vanished, slimes have crept into the quarry, the wolves have grown bolder, two more waystones on the road went dark this summer, and the Moot wants Lyra to marry the stone.
**Rumored.** The bandits are fouling the ward. The stranger is draining it. It is just a bad year.
**The truth (hidden), in two layers.**
- *Local.* Nobody tends the Wellstone now, and the crack down its face, which began in the Greywinter, is widening. The village blames the grey for that crack. In truth it was the grey and blood: Rowan's spear drew blood on the boundary stones that night, and violence inside the bell-reach thins the ward. Maren suspected as much; she asked him, and he said no. Under the quarry, the Weeping Cut is waking again. Bram's stakes hold its door but not the water that seeps out under it, and that water is what crawls in the quarry pools. *Clues:* a hitch in the Wellstone's hum at dusk that only Lyra hears; slimes in the quarry pools but not in the other rain-pools along that side of the hem; Bram's stakes sweating in dry weather.
- *The whole world.* Every wardstone belongs to a verse of the Song, and the Song is failing as its Keepers wear out (9.8). Every ward in Halcyre is thinning, and Owlsend's went dark at midsummer. *Clues:* Owlsend's lamps; the waystones dying along the road; the blue light in the ruins pulsing in time with the Wellstone's hum.
- *Taken together.* Hearthvale's ward is thinning, but more slowly than it would without Maren. Her fresh voice steadied this verse for a season, and the ruins' blue light is that verse waking. A wardwright married to the Wellstone would hold Hearthvale for years. Only mending the Song would hold every ward.

### 5. Is Lyra brightborn?

**Known.** She was born half a year after the Brightfall to a mother of fifty-two. Her hair grew in blue. Her magic woke early and strong, and her light is violet where most is white.
**Rumored.** Drift-touched. A changeling. A blessing. "Wenna went out in the brightfall" (Petra has never said this aloud).
**The truth (hidden).** Wenna asked, on the ninth night of the Brightfall, and the Drift answered, because the Drift answers without meaning anything by it. Lyra is brightborn. Her hair, the color of her light, its strength and her ear for the Song all come from that night, which is why she has always heard the Wellstone louder than anyone else. When she was born her eyes glowed and every candle in the room leaned toward her, and Petra pinched the candles out. None of it makes her one hair less Hobb and Wenna's daughter. One more thing, for the author only: the brightborn can hear the Song, and a brightborn voice is the kind of voice a failing verse wants.

### 6. The Greywinter count, and Alder's vote

**Known.** Nineteen years ago the Moot let fourteen people in from Brindlecombe "on the lamplighter's count" and turned the rest away. Many died on the road.
**Rumored.** The stone could not have borne more. The Moot was cowardly. Hobb Ambrel froze.
**The truth (hidden).** The stone had places for only a few new flames, so the real question was how many Brindlecombe souls Hearthvale's own households could take in under their flames (Section 1). Hobb kept the flame-count and the candle stores, so he went house to house in the grey and counted roofs, food stores and willing households. His answer was every soul, if families shared their flames: hungry, but alive till spring. Reeve Osric Ashby was afraid of a starving winter and of sixty strangers out of the grey. He told his son, the clerk, to read the number as *fourteen*: the children, who could be taken in without argument. Alder did. Aveline was at the ward line with the dying, not at the Moot. Hobb had gone three nights without sleep in the grey, which makes people forget, and for nineteen years he has believed he said fourteen. He half-remembers a bigger number and cannot trust it. Sabeth believes it was Hobb's count, and so does Varek. Only Alder knows.

**Alder's vote.** This summer, at the ward line, Alder spoke against letting the stranger in. At midnight, when the Moot broke up undecided, he went to the well alone. Standing there, he heard the Wellstone hum toward the ward line, where the stranger lay in the rain, and found he could not do it twice. He had read *fourteen* once and sent dozens of people back into the grey, and he would not turn away a second found one. At dawn he changed his vote, knowing it spent one of the stone's four free places. Petra saw him at the well, weeping. He has told no one why.

### 7. Owlsend — a main thread

**Known.** Owlsend lies three days north, up the track past the fork. On clear nights its lamps could be seen from the shoulder of the Hollowcrown. At midsummer, Hess says, they could not.
**Rumored.** A long grey. The bandits. Hess's eyes. "Somebody ought to say it out loud." (Hess.)
**The truth (hidden).** Owlsend's ward failed in a long grey at midsummer. Twenty-three people survived in its cellars: seven households, with five road-candles still lit. Before winter they will walk south to the nearest live ward, which is Hearthvale's.

**The author's schedule.**
- *Before the Sheaf Moot.* Cinderwick carters at the fork tell Garrick that a runner came down the north track asking the way to the nearest lit ward. He tells Elda, who tells everyone.
- *The night of the Sheaf Moot* (or as close to it as the game allows). The dusk bell is ringing for the dance when a girl of fourteen reaches the ward line on the north track with a road-candle. She is **Ketty Brask**, daughter of Owlsend's dead wardrunner. Four days behind her come the rest, led by her grandfather **Gethin Brask**, Owlsend's chapel keeper, a big, slow man who has buried half his village.
- *The emergency Moot that follows.* It replays the Greywinter with a margin of three flames. The stone bears forty-one, thirty-eight burn, and the stranger's flame took the fourth free place. Every old wound comes into the open at once:
  - Hobb will be asked to count again.
  - Alder must decide whether to lie again.
  - Hess could free a place by joining the Morrow flame to Sabeth's, and must decide whether to let Davin's flame go out.
  - Varek's band, Brindlecombe's turned-away, will watch from the hill to see what Hearthvale does this time, and Sabeth's two loyalties meet.
  - The stone question comes back at its sharpest. A married Wellstone bears four or five flames more, so if Lyra marries it, every Owlsend household gets a place of its own. She will still decide by what is good for her.

  The game must leave other answers open too: households taking people in under their flames, Hess's choice, the stranger reading the Wellstone's script, or Lyra's waystone-craft lighting a lamp at the ward line.

### 8. Why the Song broke (for the author only)

The Lanternwrights did not make magic. They found it loose, as it is now, and the Song was their answer: a working that held the Drift still across Halcyre. It was laid out in verses, and each verse was a web of stones (verse-houses, wardstones, waystones, waygates) held by singers. The Song was always paid for in singers. In every verse, a few Keepers gave themselves to it: they entered through a waygate and stayed, held out of time, singing. A verse sung by people who chose it holds. One sung by the unwilling frays. For centuries the Lanternwrights honored their Keepers above kings, and there were always enough of them.

In their last age the Lanternwrights grew rich and comfortable and stopped choosing it. They began binding others to sing in their place: prisoners, debtors, foundlings, the brightborn. The bound verses frayed and broke one after another, and in a single winter the whole Song came apart. That was the Unlighting. The few verses that survived, thinly, were the ones whose Keepers stayed by choice, like the verse of the Sunken Ruins.

So "mending the Song" would mean finding willing singers for every verse, which is to say asking people to vanish. It is the stone question at the size of the world, and the old answer to it is the one Lyra already lives by: what is given unwillingly does not hold. Nobody alive knows this. Maren had begun to guess. The deepest carvings in the ruins say it plainly to anyone who can read them.

## 10. Places

### On the map

- **Hearthvale** — Three hundred and twelve years of woodsmoke and bread around a kettle-fire, a well and a chapel. Every chimney smokes from first frost to last. Thirty-eight flames, about a hundred and fifty people.
- **Town Square** — The well, with the Wellstone humming at the bottom under an iron-banded cap that Bram re-forged last year and is re-hinging now. The notice board, where the Moot posts its business and Pip posts "LOST: BISCUIT (HE IS NOT LOST)". Benches, two market stalls, and the lamps Hobb lights first. The chapel stands on the north side. Children may not play on the well-cap. They do.
- **Your Cottage (the Wayhouse)** — Off the square's south-east corner, toward the road: the founders' house for anyone the ward lets in. Two beds, a chest with the stranger's grey clothes in it, a table, Lyra's desk and her locked trunk, Jory Fallow's books, Hobb's lamp at the door, and Biscuit on the step.
- **The Ember Kettle** — The inn, the Moot hall (in the back room) and the village's living room. Its hearth is Gerda Mead's founding fire, never once out in three hundred years. Maren's room at the top is kept as she left it, and a lantern burns in the top window every night. Lyra's old room under the eaves is still hers whenever she wants it, and Pip's cot is in the room below. Elda's strongbox is under the stairs.
- **Ironhand Smithy** — East of the square. Abel Ironhand lit the forge fifty-three years ago, and it has never gone cold. The anvil sets the village's hours. There is a tin box under it.
- **Gale's Goods** — West of the square. Garrick's shop sells potions, bread at six coppers, lanterns, berries, and anything else he can get onto a cart. A framed map to a well hangs behind the counter.
- **Chapel of the Hearth** — On the north side of the square. Thirty-eight family flames burn on the altar, and the candles lean toward the stranger whenever they kneel. The road-candle burns in the window, and Hanna Ironhand's candle is lit every week. There is a bench where the stranger slept through the long sleep, and Pip's hideout is behind the woodpile.
- **Fennick's Herbs** — Beside Gale's Goods: drying racks, silverleaf, mint, three generations of jars, and the green book. It smells like summer in every season.
- **Guardhouse** — South-west of the square: the watch's post, Rowan's home, and Wys's cot. A rack of spears, a cell nobody has used in years, and a map-table with the bandit camp circled three times. Herb bundles on the step, and a flat roof where someone plays the flute.
- **Reed Farmhouse and Reed Farm** — West of the village. Six generations of Reeds. Turnips and wheat, Duchess and her hens, Sir Barnaby the scarecrow, boar-churned fences, and a field the whole village eats from.
- **Whisperwood** — The old forest north of the village. Its near edge is inside the ward, and Wenna's hives stand there among the lindens. Farther out, where the ward thins into the hem, is the **Moonpetal Glade**, where the biggest moonpetals open at night and the post reads *"M.A. was here — look up."* Deeper in stands the heart oak, hung with ribbons and charms. Wolves, boars and deer roam the deep wood, and past the bell-reach there are things nobody should meet after dark. The path to the Hollowcrown starts here.
- **Stonecut Quarry** — North-west, in the hem. It gave the stone that built Hearthvale and the iron for Bram's forge. Slimes crawl in its rain-pools. Under its north face, behind Bram's iron stakes, is the Weeping Cut.
- **Bandit Camp** — In the hills north-east of Mirror Lake, above the Cinderwick fork and outside the ward. Tents, a fire that never quite goes out, Varek's chest with the rusted key in it, and crates stamped with the chapel's seal and a Cinderwick carter's mark. It looks down on both roads. On the track below stands the old boundary stone where Garrick leaves his purse and where Sabeth met her brother.
- **Mirror Lake** — East of the village, so still it holds the whole sky. Corran's dock is on the western shore, with his net-shed at its landward end and the lake campfire nearby. The old ferry-house Tobias is buying stands at the south end, where the river Bright runs out. Slimes gather along the shore where the ward thins, and something at the bottom hums.
- **Sunken Ruins** — The walled verse-house in the south-western marsh. Outside: the sheer wall, the sinkhole where its low stretch fell in, the locked iron gate, and the empty keeper's lodge. Inside: the upper halls with the old pilgrims' shrine, fallen pillars, blue ruin-lichen, glow crystals, stone that rings, and a statue holding up a lantern whose worn inscription reads *"...the light that goes before..."*. Bats come out at dusk, skeletons walk the halls, and gravelight glows after dark. At the foot of the stair are a black altar with fresh scuffs where Maren knelt, her journal, and the waygate.
- **South Meadow** — South of the village: flowers, rabbits, and puddle-slimes after drift-weather. This is where Wenna stood in the brightfall. The hem runs through its far side, and it is the best place in the valley to watch the ruins' lights, which is why Pip and Tansy go there.
- **King's Road** — It runs along the village's southern edge from west to east, crosses the stream at the old bridge, and then splits: the Bright Road goes south-east, and the King's Road bends north to the fork. One waystone in three is still lit. Wolves run at dusk where the road passes under the edge of the wood, and that is where Lyra and the stranger stood together.
